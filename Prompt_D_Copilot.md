### Response

The manuscripts show no single, unified theory of intelligence, but they contain a pattern of remarks across the Nachlass that reveal how Wittgenstein understood what we call “being intelligent.” The core idea is consistent throughout: intelligence is not an inner mental faculty but something displayed in rule‑governed human practices.

Core takeaway

Wittgenstein treats intelligence as a grammatical concept, not a mental mechanism. In the manuscripts, it refers to abilities shown in action, especially in language‑games, rule‑following, and mastery of techniques.

 What the manuscripts actually say (across MSS 101–182, 105–122, 137–174)

1\. Intelligence as ability in practice

Across the Bände (MSS 105–122), Wittgenstein repeatedly frames psychological concepts—including “understanding,” “cleverness,” and “thinking”—as criteria in behavior, not inner states.

“Intelligence” appears only as a description of how someone proceeds in a practice.

The manuscripts emphasize that we see intelligence in how someone continues a series, grasps a rule, or responds appropriately.

This is supported by the Nachlass’s treatment of psychological concepts in the manuscripts underlying Philosophical Grammar and Remarks on the Philosophy of Psychology.

2\. Critique of mentalistic explanations

In later manuscripts (MSS 137–138, 169–174), Wittgenstein rejects the idea that intelligence is a hidden mental engine.

He argues that positing an inner faculty “explains nothing.”

Intelligence is not a cause but a label for a pattern of behavior within a form of life.

These manuscripts feed into Last Writings on the Philosophy of Psychology.

3\. Rule‑following as the paradigm of intelligence

Manuscripts connected to Philosophical Grammar (MSS 114–115, MS 140) use examples like:

continuing a numerical series,

understanding a command,

grasping a technique.

These are not signs of an inner ability but public criteria for what we call “intelligence.”

The Nachlass catalog confirms these manuscripts as sources for Wittgenstein’s rule‑following discussions.

4\. Intelligence as mastery of a technique

In the 1930s manuscripts (MSS 113–122), Wittgenstein describes understanding as mastery of a technique.

Intelligence is shown in the ability to apply a technique correctly.

He contrasts this with the mistaken idea that intelligence is a “state of mind.”

These manuscripts form the backbone of Philosophical Remarks and Philosophical Grammar.

5\. Forms of life determine what counts as intelligence

Later manuscripts (MSS 169–174) emphasize that “intelligent behavior” is relative to human forms of life.

A creature counts as intelligent only if its actions fit our shared human criteria.

Intelligence is not absolute; it is context‑dependent.

These manuscripts feed into On Certainty and Last Writings on Psychology.

 Synthesis

Across the Nachlass, Wittgenstein’s manuscripts consistently treat intelligence as:

A normative, public concept describing how someone participates competently in human practices—not an inner mental faculty.

This view is spread across notebooks, Bände, and late psychological manuscripts, but it is remarkably stable: intelligence is shown, not hidden.

### Response

Wittgenstein does not treat intelligence as a hidden inner substance or measurable mental mechanism. Instead, he repeatedly examines how words like “intelligence,” “thinking,” “understanding,” and “knowing” are actually used in human life and language-games. For example, he remarks that some people, when hearing an isolated word, would immediately form a sentence with it, and that we might call this a sign of intelligence, while the inability to do so might count as dullness. Intelligence here appears as a capacity manifested in practice, not as an occult inner state. [Ms-130\_262[2]]

He is skeptical of psychological or scientific attempts to capture intelligence through tests or mechanisms. He writes that intelligence tests certainly test “something,” but not what we ordinarily call intelligence or presence of mind. He criticizes such approaches for remaining on the surface and missing what is essential. [Ms-108\_134[1]]

Wittgenstein also distinguishes different forms of cleverness. He speaks of “the stages between instinctive cunning and thought-out cunning,” noting that even an “idiot” might act cleverly without being capable of planning. Our judgments about intelligence depend on behavior, expression, and what we call thinking ability, but he warns that we do not really know what is “going on inside” another person. [Ms-137\_59b[4]]

In several remarks he rejects the idea that thinking or understanding are mysterious inner processes. He argues that calling meaning or understanding a “mental activity” easily misleads us. [Ms-130\_20[3]] He repeatedly compares understanding with abilities such as playing chess or speaking a language: understanding is shown in use, mastery, and practice rather than in introspectively observable inner events. [Ms-131\_110[3]et120[1]; Ms-140\_13r[2]; Ms-109\_179[3]]

This also shapes his reflections on intelligent behavior. He asks whether there could be people who cannot calculate mentally or learn silently, yet are otherwise very intelligent and in no sense feeble-minded. [Ms-135\_4v[2]] Likewise, he says that the ability to think is a kind of “mental mobility,” closely connected with the capacity to use language. [Ms-136\_61b[5]et62a[1]]

Wittgenstein often criticizes the temptation to imagine hidden “mental machinery” behind intelligent activity. He writes that understanding as a “disposition of the soul or brain” is not what concerns him philosophically. [Ms-109\_186[4]] He also warns against treating thought as a mysterious ethereal process accompanying speech and action. [Ms-136\_61b[4]; Ms-114\_78v[2]] Instead, he emphasizes criteria in outward practice: how someone speaks, acts, follows rules, solves problems, or participates in shared forms of life.

His remarks on genius and talent also illuminate his conception of intelligence. He distinguishes wanting to think from having a talent for thinking. [Ms-127\_224[3]et225[1]] He further says that genius is “talent in which character expresses itself.” [Ms-136\_59a[4]et59b[1]; Ms-168\_4v[2]et5r[1]] Intelligence or talent alone therefore does not amount to greatness; what matters is the whole human being and the form of life expressed through thought and action.

Overall, Wittgenstein’s manuscripts portray intelligence not as a hidden mental substance but as something visible in capacities, language use, behavior, learning, responsiveness, and participation in human practices. He resists both introspective mysticism and reductive psychological measurement, insisting that philosophical clarity comes from examining the grammar and use of concepts such as “thinking,” “understanding,” and “knowing.”
