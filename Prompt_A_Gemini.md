### Response

Ludwig Wittgenstein did not write a systematic treatise specifically about "intelligence" as a psychological trait. Instead, his remarks on the topic focus on the **limits, traps, and proper function of human intellect**, particularly regarding how language can distort our thinking.

His views on intelligence, cleverness, and intellectual vanity can be understood through several key themes:

**1. The "Bewitchment" of the Intellect**

Wittgenstein’s most famous statement regarding intelligence appears in his later work, *Philosophical Investigations*:

*"Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language."*

He believed that intellectual problems (especially in philosophy) do not arise because humans are unintelligent, but rather because we are hypnotized by the surface grammar of our own language. We fall into conceptual traps—creating pseudo-problems about reality, mind, and meaning—because language forces pictures upon us that capture our intellect. Intelligence, for Wittgenstein, must constantly struggle against its own linguistic illusions.

**2. The Danger of "Cleverness" and Abstraction**

Wittgenstein was deeply suspicious of purely academic cleverness or intellectual posturing. He valued intellectual honesty and simplicity over dry intellectualism, warning against staying too long in abstract realms:

- **On "barren heights":** *"Never stay up on the barren heights of cleverness, but come down into the green valleys of silliness."* 
- **On intellectual fertility:** *"A philosopher always finds more grass to feed upon in the valleys of stupidity than on the arid heights of intelligence."* 

By "valleys of silliness," Wittgenstein meant ordinary human behavior, common sense, and the willingness to look foolish or say something "nonsensical" in order to break through rigid intellectual prejudices.

**3. The Necessity of Silliness and Mistakes**

True intellectual progress or breakthrough, in Wittgenstein's view, often requires breaking away from rigid, hyper-rational constraints. He famously noted:

*"If people never did silly things nothing intelligent would ever get done."*

He suggested that raw intellectual calculation is not enough; deep understanding often springs from unorthodox, eccentric, or seemingly "silly" modes of thought that challenge established norms.

**4. Intellect vs. Will**

Wittgenstein also argued that the hardest intellectual and philosophical problems are not solved by simply being smarter or gathering more technical information, but by a transformation of character and attitude. In his manuscripts (*Culture and Value* / *Big Typescript*), he wrote:

*"What has to be overcome is not difficulty of the intellect but of the will."*

Cleverness alone cannot solve existential or philosophical knots. Overcoming confusion requires moral and psychological honesty—the willingness to give up intellectual pride and look plainly at what is right in front of us.

### Response

Wittgenstein does not treat intelligence as a hidden inner substance or measurable mental mechanism. Instead, he repeatedly examines how we use concepts like “thinking,” “understanding,” “intuition,” and “intelligence” in actual human practices and language-games.

He is skeptical of attempts to define intelligence through psychological tests or inner mental states. He remarks that intelligence tests may indeed test “something,” but not necessarily what we ordinarily call intelligence or presence of mind. He says such tests remain on the surface and miss what is essential. [Ms-108\_134\[1\]]

In one remark, Wittgenstein imagines that some people, when hearing an isolated word, immediately form a sentence with it, whereas others do not; the first reaction could count as a sign of intelligence and the second as dullness. [Ms-130\_262\[2\]; Ts-229\_237\[2\]; Ts-245\_170\[6\]] Here intelligence is connected with a capacity for linguistic responsiveness and flexible use of language rather than with an occult mental faculty.

He also resists identifying intelligence with particular cognitive skills. He asks whether there could not be people unable to calculate mentally or learn silent reading, who nevertheless are otherwise very intelligent and in no sense “feeble-minded.” [Ms-135\_4v\[2\]; Ts-229\_420\[5\]; Ts-245\_301\[5\]] This suggests that intelligence cannot be reduced to a single standardized ability.

Wittgenstein distinguishes between instinctive cleverness and reflective cleverness. He notes that even an “idiot” might behave cleverly although we would not think him capable of planning. He warns, however, that our judgments about what is “going on” in such a person are speculative and based on outward behavior, expressions, and capacities. [Ms-137\_59b\[4\]; Ts-232\_759\[4\]] This fits his broader rejection of hidden mental explanations detached from criteria in practice.

He often connects intelligence with mastery of language and forms of life. For example, he says the ability to think is a kind of “mental mobility,” but immediately asks whether the ability to use language is not itself mental mobility. [[Ms-136\_61b\[5\]et62a\1\]] Similarly, he emphasizes that understanding and knowing are tied to abilities and criteria shown in use, not to private inner experiences. [Ms-142\_134\[2\]; Ts-220\_106\[2\]]

Wittgenstein also links philosophical ability to a special sensitivity to grammar. He says the capacity for philosophy consists in the ability to receive a strong and lasting impression from a fact of grammar. [Ts-211\_8\[1\]; Ms-153a\_62r\[3\]; Ms-111\_11\[5\]] Philosophical intelligence, in this sense, is not technical brilliance but a responsiveness to conceptual distinctions.

Finally, he sometimes speaks critically about “higher intellect.” When someone says that only a higher intellect could grasp something, Wittgenstein replies by asking for the grammar of the expression “higher intellect”: what could such an intellect grasp, and under what circumstances do we say that an intellect grasps something? [[Ms-113\_80v\[4\]et81r\1\]; Ts-211\_642\[3\]; Ts-212\_XIX-140-5[1]] He thereby shifts attention away from metaphysical speculation about superior minds toward the concrete use of our concepts.

Overall, Wittgenstein treats intelligence less as an inner entity than as something expressed in human capacities, practices, responsiveness, judgment, and language. He is consistently suspicious of theories that try to explain intelligence by appealing to hidden mental mechanisms or abstract psychological entities.
