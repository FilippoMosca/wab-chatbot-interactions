### Response

Wittgenstein is generally skeptical about saying that a machine literally thinks, while his remarks on Turing’s machines are more about how we represent calculation and rule-following than about proving machine intelligence. In short, the first issue is about the grammar of mental predicates like “think,” and the second is about the status of formal procedures and models of computation.

Machine thinking

In Wittgenstein’s philosophy, whether something “thinks” is not settled by hidden inner machinery alone; it depends on how the concept of thinking is used in our practices. That is why he resists the idea that one could simply inspect a mechanism and conclude that it thinks in the same sense a person does.

So his remarks on machines can sound deflationary: a machine may behave in ways we call intelligent, but that does not automatically mean the word “think” applies to it in the ordinary human sense.

Turing machines

By contrast, Wittgenstein’s remarks on Turing’s machines concern calculation, formal procedures, and what it means to follow a rule. The relevant issue is not “Does this device have thoughts?” but “How do we understand a machine-like process that carries out a calculation?”.

A Turing machine is an abstract model of effective computation, and Wittgenstein’s discussions of it are often read as focused on the relation between mathematical description and actual human calculative activity. One source notes that he even described Turing’s machines as “humans who calculate,” which suggests he was treating the model as tied to human practice rather than as a decisive theory of mindedness.

Main contrast

The contrast is therefore this:

On machines and thinking, Wittgenstein asks whether the concept “think” legitimately extends to machines at all, and he tends to warn against superficial extensions of mental vocabulary.

On Turing machines, he is engaging a model of computation and rule-following, where the question is about the nature of calculation, not directly about consciousness or mentality.

So the first set of remarks is philosophical and semantic about mentality, while the second is logical and methodological about computation.

Shared point

Still, the two topics meet in one important place: Wittgenstein is wary of treating a formal model as if it automatically settled a philosophical question. A Turing machine can clarify what a calculation is, but it does not by itself answer whether a machine thinks.

### Response

Wittgenstein’s remarks on whether a machine can think are mainly grammatical and conceptual investigations into how the word “thinking” is used. He repeatedly rejects the idea that “A machine cannot think” is an empirical discovery. Rather, we ordinarily apply the word “think” to humans and to what resembles them; the issue concerns the use of concepts, not hidden facts about mechanisms. [Ms-129\_177[4]et178[1]; Ts-230a\_21[5]; Ms-165\_62[2]] He therefore compares the question “Can a machine think?” with “Can a machine have pains?” and argues that the answer depends on what we mean by “having pains.” A machine could in principle behave exactly like a person in pain, and we may even choose to call a body “a machine that has pains”; but in the first-person use of “I have toothache,” the machine “has no place.” [Ts-211\_156[2]; Ms-114\_76r[2]; Ts-213\_213r[2]et212v[1]]

In the early 1930s he sometimes formulates the issue by asking whether thought is a specifically human or organic process, and whether there could be a “thought prosthesis.” [Ts-213\_IIIr[11]; Ts-212\_VI-51FCr[1]] He often answers by dissolving the contrast between human and mechanical performance. Since thinking may consist in writing, calculating, drawing, or operating with symbols, a machine could “think” insofar as it performs those activities. [Ts-303\_8[3]; Ms-145\_24[3]et25[1]; Ms-114\_125r[2]et125v[1]] Yet he also warns that this way of speaking tempts us into a false picture: we begin to imagine thought as a hidden process analogous to weaving or to the workings of a mechanism. [Ts-211\_118[6]et119[1]; Ts-212\_VI-49-4[1]; Ms-110\_2[3]et3[1]] His criticism is therefore directed less against machines than against the assumption that thinking is an occult inner mechanism.

This is why Wittgenstein frequently attacks the picture of thought as a concealed process inside the head, comparable to a machine in a closed box. [Ms-110\_40[4]; Ts-211\_161[3]; Ts-213\_220r[6]] He says that thinking is not to be understood as the operation of a hidden mechanism whose interior must be inspected. [Ms-110\_4[5]; Ts-212\_VI-52-3[2]] The temptation to speak this way comes from misleading analogies embedded in language. [Ms-109\_174[2]; Ms-145\_86[3]]

His later remarks on Turing machines are more specific and much less metaphysical. Wittgenstein says that Turing’s “machines” are really human calculators following rules. [Ts-229\_448[1]; Ms-135\_59v[2]et60r[1]; Ts-245\_319[3]] He interprets Turing machines as calculative procedures expressible as games governed by rules. What interests him philosophically is not an intelligent mechanism hidden behind the calculation, but the grammar of rule-following and the possibility that rule-governed procedures can generate nonsensical instructions. [Ts-229\_448[1]; Ts-245\_319[3]] He gives the example of a command like “continue in the same way” becoming meaningless when the procedure runs into a circle; the command only has sense in certain contexts. [Ts-229\_448[1]; Ms-135\_59v[2]et60r[1]]

The contrast is important. In the remarks on machine thinking, Wittgenstein is chiefly dismantling philosophical pictures: the notion of an inner mental mechanism, the contrast between living and dead processes, or the assumption that thought is a mysterious accompaniment to speech and action. [Ts-213\_216r[5]; Ms-129\_91[2]et92[1]et93[1]; Ts-227a\_197[6]et198[1]] In the remarks on Turing machines, by contrast, he treats the machine as a formalized human practice of calculation. Turing’s machines are not mysterious minds but idealized human calculators operating according to rules. [Ts-229\_448[1]; Ts-245\_319[3]]

At the same time, there is continuity between the two discussions. In both cases Wittgenstein resists the idea that thinking is identified by an inner occult process. Whether discussing machines, human calculators, or Turing procedures, he redirects attention to language-games, criteria, rule-following, and forms of activity. [Ms-129\_177[4]et178[1]; Ts-229\_448[1]; Ms-145\_24[3]et25[1]] He consistently treats “thinking” as a concept whose application depends on human practices rather than on discovering a hidden mental engine inside either humans or machines.
