### Response

Between 1931 and 1933, Wittgenstein does not develop a theory of intelligence in the psychological sense. Instead, he makes a series of remarks—mostly in his diaries, Culture and Value, manuscripts that later became the Big Typescript (TS 213), and the beginning of the Blue Book—that treat intelligence as something closely connected with clarity, honesty, originality, and the capacity to overcome philosophical confusion, rather than raw intellectual power. 

Several recurring themes emerge. 

1\. Intelligence is not the same as philosophical greatness 

Wittgenstein repeatedly distinguishes sheer intellectual ability (Intelligenz, Begabung) from philosophical insight. For him, someone may be extremely clever yet philosophically blind because philosophy demands a transformation of one's way of seeing rather than technical brilliance. Throughout this period he increasingly portrays philosophy as work on oneself: Philosophy is not primarily a matter of discovering new truths but of overcoming temptations created by language. This shift is especially visible in the manuscripts leading to the Blue Book (1933), where philosophical problems are presented as confusions rather than puzzles requiring greater intelligence. 

2\. Character matters more than intelligence 

One of Wittgenstein's most characteristic remarks from this period is that philosophical progress depends more on character than on intellect. He repeatedly suggests that the real obstacle in philosophy is not lack of intelligence but: vanity, the desire to impress, impatience, intellectual pride, the inability to acknowledge one's own confusions. This idea appears throughout the notebooks of 1930–1932 and later selections in Culture and Value. Philosophy requires a kind of moral discipline. 

3\. Originality is not inventiveness 

During these years Wittgenstein also reflects on originality. He denies that originality consists in producing ingenious theories. Instead, originality lies in seeing what everyone else overlooks. This explains why he often praises simplicity over cleverness. The genuinely intelligent philosopher is not the one who constructs elaborate systems but the one who can dissolve unnecessary complications. 

4\. Intelligence has limits 

Another recurring thought is that every thinker has natural limits. Wittgenstein often writes with unusual severity about his own intellectual capacities. Around this period he remarks that he frequently reaches the limits of what he can think clearly and that philosophical work consists in recognizing these limits honestly rather than pretending to surpass them. This self-critical attitude is characteristic of the diary entries from 1930–1932. 

5\. The danger of cleverness 

He is suspicious of mere cleverness (Klugheit). A clever argument can easily become another source of philosophical illusion. Good philosophy is therefore not the display of intelligence but the removal of misunderstanding. This attitude anticipates the famous metaphor of philosophy as therapy that becomes central in the Blue Book and later the Philosophical Investigations. 

6\. Intelligence and language 

By 1933, Wittgenstein increasingly locates what appears to be "thinking" within our mastery of language. The Blue Book begins to reject the idea that thinking is an inner process whose quality depends on mental power. Instead, understanding depends on learning to use words correctly within our practices. Consequently, many philosophical puzzles attributed to insufficient intelligence are actually misunderstandings of grammar. 

A representative remark 

Although slightly earlier (1931), one remark from Culture and Value captures his attitude well: "Working in philosophy is really more a working on oneself." This does not deny the importance of intelligence, but it relocates philosophical excellence from IQ to intellectual honesty, self-discipline, and the ability to see clearly. 

Overall picture (1931–1933) 

If one had to summarize Wittgenstein's conception of intelligence during these years, it would be something like this: Intelligence is necessary but insufficient for philosophy. Character is often more important than brilliance. The greatest philosophical obstacle is not stupidity but self-deception. Philosophical clarity comes from changing one's way of looking, not from becoming more clever. Genuine intelligence manifests itself in the ability to resist misleading pictures created by language. This period marks the transition from the logical rigor of the Tractatus to the therapeutic conception of philosophy that culminates in the Blue Book (1933–34) and later the Philosophical Investigations.

### Response

Between 1931 and 1933, Wittgenstein repeatedly criticizes the idea that intelligence, thought, or understanding are mysterious inner processes or powers hidden behind language and action. He examines the grammar of expressions such as “higher intellect,” “thinking,” and “understanding,” and argues that philosophical confusion arises when we imagine thought as an occult mental mechanism.

He directly questions the idea of a “higher intellect” that could grasp what human beings cannot. When someone says, “Only the human intellect cannot grasp this, a higher one could,” Wittgenstein replies that one must explain the grammar of the expression “higher intellect”: what would count as such an intellect grasping something, and under what experiences we would say that understanding has occurred. He concludes that “the description of grasping is the grasping itself.” [Ms-113\_80v[4]et81r[1]; Ts-211\_642[3]; Ts-212\_XIX-140-5[1]; Ts-213\_767r[5]]

Wittgenstein also resists the idea that thinking is a hidden process inside the head. He writes that saying “thinking goes on in the head” means only that the head has something to do with thinking, and adds that one may equally well say “I think with the pen on paper.” [Ms-110\_40[3]; Ts-211\_161[2]; Ts-212\_VI-52-2[1]; Ts-213\_220r[5]] He even remarks that he often thinks with the pen, because his head “often knows nothing of what my hand writes.” [Ms-112\_58r[5]] Elsewhere he says he sometimes anticipates his own thinking in writing. [Ms-112\_50v[2]]

This criticism extends to the conception of thought as a specifically mental or spiritual activity. Wittgenstein argues that calling thinking a “mental process” causes confusion because we imagine something analogous to a chemical or physiological process. [Ts-211\_409[1]; Ts-212\_VIII-62-10[1]; Ts-213\_277r[3]] He says that to describe thinking as an activity of the “mind” in the same sense in which speaking is an activity of the mouth is “a travesty.” [Ts-211\_119[2]; Ts-212\_VI-52-3[1]; Ts-213\_220r[7]et221r[1]] The idea of thought as occurring in an enclosed inner realm gives it an “occult” character. [Ts-213\_220r[4]]

Instead of treating thought as an invisible process, Wittgenstein connects it with the use of symbols, language, and calculi. He says that “thinking” can consist in writing, reading, drawing, or calculating. [Ts-213\_220r[2]; Ts-303\_8[3]] He defines thinking as the use and operation of symbols. [Ms-114\_77r[2]] He repeatedly compares understanding a sentence to mastering a calculus or being able to continue a numerical series. [Ts-213\_143r[4]; Ts-212\_IV-38-16[1]]

Wittgenstein rejects “intuitive thinking” conceived as a timeless grasp of a whole. He compares “intuitive thinking” to “a chess game brought into the form of a permanent, unchanging state,” something incoherent. [Ms-111\_6[2]; Ts-211\_4[2]; Ts-213\_158r[2]; Ms-114\_77v[2]] Thought is instead discursive and unfolds in time. [Ms-110\_296[6]et297[1]; Ts-211\_310[5]]

He also opposes the idea that understanding depends on hidden psychological accompaniments. He insists that “nothing about thought is essentially private.” [Ts-212\_VI-53-3[1]; Ts-213\_222r[6]] Understanding and meaning must be visible in the practice itself, not inferred from occult inner states. [Ts-212\_XI-81-7[1]; Ts-213\_383r[2]] Thus he says that if the process of understanding is to count as understanding, “understanding must find expression in the fact of understanding.” [Ts-212\_XI-81-7[1]; Ts-213\_383r[2]]

In remarks on philosophy and mathematics, Wittgenstein contrasts genuine philosophical sensitivity with intellectual bluntness. He says that the philosopher notices changes in the “style” of a proof that contemporary mathematicians pass over “with dull faces.” A future mathematics, he thinks, would depend on a “higher sensitivity” directed toward clarity rather than invention for its own sake. [Ms-113\_22r[2]; Ts-212\_XVII-122-21[1]etXVII-122-22[1]; Ts-213\_643r[3]] Likewise, he says the capacity for philosophy lies in the ability to receive a strong and lasting impression from a fact of grammar. [Ms-153a\_62r[3]; Ts-211\_8[1]; Ts-212\_XII-90-3[1]]

Taken together, these remarks show Wittgenstein moving away from any theory of intelligence as an inner faculty or mysterious mental substance. Intelligence and understanding are treated instead as manifested in language, action, symbolic practices, and forms of life. Philosophical clarity comes not from discovering hidden mental machinery but from examining how concepts such as “thinking,” “understanding,” and “intellect” are actually used.
